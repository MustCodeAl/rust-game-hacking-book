This comparison was checked on **October 5, 2026**. Products change their
requirements, supported games, and detection systems. The examples show
documented architecture, rather than a ranking or a guarantee that any
particular match is free of cheating.

## VALORANT and Vanguard

Riot describes Vanguard as a game-time client component and a kernel
driver. Its traditional mode starts the driver at boot so protection can
observe the environment before the game client starts.

On June 24, 2026, Riot announced optional **on-demand** sessions for
sufficiently secured PCs. In this mode, protection starts with a Riot
game and runs while it is being played. The published prerequisites include
an appropriate Windows version, Secure Boot, a TPM, virtualization-based
security, code-integrity protection, and an IOMMU. Boot-managed operation
remains a different mode; it is inaccurate to describe every Vanguard
installation as always starting at boot.

VALORANT also illustrates why client protection cannot replace server
design. Its server-side visibility policy limits hidden information sent
to a client. Local privilege and server disclosure are separate controls.

## Counter-Strike and Valve's layers

**VAC**, Valve Anti-Cheat, contributes client-side detection and server
enforcement of VAC status. The game still needs its own validation and
review systems. The public material does not provide a complete recipe for
VAC or VAC Live detections, so do not infer one from a product label.

Valve's August 19, 2024 update described initial limited-match testing of
VacNet 3.0 and asked for details of incorrectly cancelled matches. That
dated announcement does not establish a universal current rollout or a
measured detection rate.

Valve's April 25, 2024 Counter-Strike 2 notes added an **Overwatch** system
for match-demo review by trusted partners. This is a dated description of
review support, not a promise that every account can access the old CS:GO
review interface.

The earlier CS:GO **Trust Factor** documentation describes matchmaking
using account and play observations, with changing factors left
undisclosed. Matchmaking risk, detection, and a reviewed sanction are
different decisions. A historical FAQ cannot establish today's internal
CS2 scoring formula.

## Call of Duty and RICOCHET

Activision's current description combines server-side systems and
investigation with a PC kernel driver. It states that the driver operates
only while a protected Call of Duty title runs, and monitors software
interacting with those titles.

Server-side protections can also affect console players through shared
services and cross-play. The presence of a PC driver does not imply that
every participant uses the same local component.

## Easy Anti-Cheat and BattlEye

Epic's **Easy Anti-Cheat** integration through Epic Online Services supports
client-server and peer-to-peer architectures. In client-server mode, game
network code carries opaque messages between the client and server
protection modules. **Opaque** means the game transports the data without
interpreting its internal format. The developer still owns game-rule
validation and must integrate the required session and message lifecycle.

BattlEye describes user-mode and kernel-mode protection, proactive
prevention, and detection. These descriptions establish parts of its
design, rather than an independent measurement of how well it protects
every supported title.

<MarginNote kind="alternative">Compare what a system sees, when it runs, and who owns the decision. Marketing names do not answer those questions alone.</MarginNote>

A useful comparison also includes supported platforms, privacy, reliability,
appeals, and the game's own authority model. Public ban counts lack the
undetected cases and legitimate population needed to calculate precision
or recall. Lesson 15.5 explains those missing denominators.
