A match can have several protection layers. The game validates its rules.
A platform provides account and service signals. A league may add its own
client requirements and review process. A community server may run plugins
with local policies. These layers need not see the same facts or impose
the same consequences.

## Know which observation a layer owns

The game server knows its accepted inventory and simulation. Client
protection sees parts of the local software environment. An account service
can know historical enforcement records. A replay reviewer sees whatever
the recording retained. Each observation has a time, scope, and source.

**Provenance** records where information came from and how it was obtained.
Keep the originating event identifier, game version, server state, and
detector version when forwarding a signal. Otherwise, a later service may
misinterpret a copied report as a new measurement.

## Three reports can describe one event

Label a toy event A. The game logs it, a plugin forwards it, and a player
report references it. That creates three notifications. Their common
origin means they still describe one event.

<Scene name="evidence-correlation" />

A later event B adds another observation. Now there are two distinct
events. Distinct does not automatically mean statistically independent:
both might have been affected by the same game bug or network condition.

**Correlation** means observations vary together. When several detectors
share inputs or assumptions, their mistakes can occur together too. Count
unique events and evaluate the combined policy on legitimate cases. Do
not turn a copied signal into extra confidence merely by routing it
through additional services.

<MarginNote kind="alternative">Preserve the original event identity across services. Combine its context before deciding how much weight it deserves.</MarginNote>

## Matchmaking risk is different from a cheating verdict

A **trust score** summarizes risk or expected match quality for a policy.
It may guide matchmaking or prioritize review. It is not necessarily an
assertion that the account cheated in a particular match.

Valve's published CS:GO Trust Factor explanation illustrates that
distinction: it describes matchmaking using account and play observations,
while leaving changing details undisclosed. An account score does not
replace a rule-specific explanation for an enforcement decision.

New accounts, shared hardware, changing devices, and unusual but permitted
play can complicate such signals. Keep the scope of a consequence tied to
the evidence and documented rules. A community server's local removal is
different from a platform-wide sanction.

## Delayed enforcement has tradeoffs

A **ban wave** applies accumulated enforcement decisions in a batch.
Grouping cases can give investigators time to validate them and make
immediate detector feedback less revealing. The delay also allows further
harm unless other controls prevent or mitigate it.

Use durable case records and preserve the rule version that made each
decision. If an update causes a false-positive cluster, pause the affected
enforcement rule, investigate its shared cause, and correct affected cases.
A general recovery process should exist before a large batch is applied.

Review queues need clear prioritization and limited data access. A report
can be valuable without being sufficient evidence for a ban. Repeated
reports from the same source or the same incident should not automatically
increase the penalty. The purpose of layering is to add useful coverage
and context, not to hide responsibility behind a majority of flags.
