Lesson 7.7 / Listening edition

Recoil, Spread, and Camera Motion

Separate input, weapon kick, random spread, camera shake and other camera motion, and recovery to understand why the view moves after a shot.

About 32 minutes

Section: What recoil is inside a program.

On screen, recoil looks like the weapon pushing the camera. In code, it is usually one or more numbers added to a view model over time.

The game may keep separate values for:

the player’s intended yaw and pitch from the mouse;

an immediate weapon kick;

a recovery amount that eases back toward zero;

random spread used by projectile calculations;

camera shake used only for presentation;

the weapon model’s animation.

Those systems can produce similar movement while controlling different things. Removing a camera kick should not be assumed to remove projectile spread, and changing the weapon animation should not be assumed to change aim.

In brief: View kick, weapon animation, and projectile spread can look related while coming from different calculations.

This diagram deliberately gives rendered view and shot direction separate paths. If two systems read the same base aim but add different offsets, changing one will not necessarily change the other.

An angle describes rotation. Yaw usually turns left and right around a vertical axis. Pitch looks up and down. Games may store degrees, radians, fixed point units, or normalized values, so the number 90.0 has meaning only after measurement.

Section: Observe one axis at a time.

In an offline AssaultCube session, log view angles while:

standing still;

moving the mouse;

firing one shot;

firing a burst;

switching weapons.

Recoil may change the same yaw and pitch values as the mouse, or it may update separate kick values that are combined later.

Log time as well as values. A single before-and-after pair shows total movement; a short time series shows whether the kick happens instantly and how recovery behaves across later frames.

Give each sample a monotonic timestamp and, when available, the frame or simulation tick. Wall-clock timestamps can jump; a monotonic clock measures elapsed duration. Each sample also needs the weapon, fire mode, and whether the shot was accepted. Otherwise a reload or dry-fire sample can be mistaken for zero recoil.

This damped-spring model explains overshoot and smooth return. Other games use a fixed curve, exponential decay, a table of weapon-specific offsets, or direct angle changes. Fit the simplest model supported by measured samples rather than assuming every recovery is a spring.

For exponential recovery, x(t) equals x₀e^(-kt). Equal fractions disappear in equal time intervals. For linear recovery, equal absolute amounts disappear. Plotting a few samples makes the difference visible.

Section: Random spread: choosing a point in a circle.

Spread adds a random offset to the direction of a shot. The offset is usually a random point inside a small circle around the aim direction, and picking that point fairly is easier to get wrong than it looks.

The obvious recipe is a random distance r equals R times u, with u chosen evenly between 0 and 1, at a random angle. It bunches the shots near the middle. Half of the values of u fall below 0.5, so half of the shots land inside the circle of radius 0.5 times R, but that circle has only (0.5)² equals 25 percent of the area. The fix is to take a square root, r equals R times square root of u, because the area inside a radius grows as the radius squared:

Narration: Choosing radius evenly makes a dense centre because small radii enclose less area. A random-looking spread is not necessarily evenly distributed across the disk.

The two values u and v come from a pseudo-random number generator. A game that seeds it with the same number gets the same “random” sequence every time, which is how replays and networked games keep every machine in agreement. Two runs that start from the same seed repeat their spread exactly, and runs that start from different seeds do not.

The spread offset is added to the shot direction, not to the camera, which is why the diagram above gives them separate paths.

Section: Camera motion: shake, follow, orbit, and zoom.

The camera has more layers than recoil. Each calculates a position or viewing direction: shake adds an offset, follow tracks a subject, orbit rotates around a point, and zoom changes the framing.

Shake is a short random offset that fades. With an amplitude of 4 pixels that fades to 0 over 0.5 s, the largest offset at 0.1 s is 4 times (1 minus 0.1 divided by 0.5) equals 3.2 pixels, at 0.25 s it is 2.0, and at 0.5 s it is 0. Many games track a “trauma” value from 0 to 1 and shake by its square, so a light hit of 0.5 shakes at only 0.25 of the maximum. Shake is presentation: it moves the picture, not the aim.

Follow cameras trail the player. A top-down 2D camera eases toward the player with the smoothing from Lesson 4.4, and often has a dead zone, a box around the screen’s centre inside which the player can move without moving the camera.

Orbit cameras circle a target. With z up, the camera sits at the target plus distance times (cos(pitch) times cos(yaw), cos(pitch) times sin(yaw), sin(pitch)) and always looks back at the target. For a target at the origin, a distance of 10, a yaw of 90 degrees, and a pitch of 30 degrees, that is 10 times (0.866 times 0, 0.866 times 1, 0.5) equals (0, 8.66, 5).

First-person cameras sit at the character’s head and take yaw and pitch from the mouse. Pitch is clamped just short of straight up or down, so the view cannot flip over ( Lesson 7.1 ). The weapon in the player’s hand, the view model, is often drawn with its own field of view, narrower than the world’s, so it does not look stretched.

Free and pan cameras are for editors: a free camera moves along its own forward and right directions, and a 2D pan camera moves opposite to the drag.

Zoom narrows the field of view for a perspective camera, which Lesson 7.1 showed scales the picture by 1.732 when it goes from 90 degrees to 60 degrees. For an orthographic camera, zooming shrinks the box of visible world, so halving its width doubles the pixels per unit.

Composition order matters for 3D rotations. Adding small yaw/pitch offsets is a common approximation, but full orientations may be matrices or quaternions. Large rotations do not generally commute: applying yaw then pitch can differ from pitch then yaw.
