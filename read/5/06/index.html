Lesson 7.6 / Listening edition

Aim Geometry and Target Selection

Connect vectors to yaw, pitch, angular error, target ranking, how animation poses a skeleton, target points, timing, and projectile motion.

About 44 minutes

Section: Break the problem into layers.

An aim-assistance experiment contains three different problems:

Snapshot — where are the camera and candidates right now?

Decision — which candidate is valid and closest to the crosshair?

Action — where and when should the verified view-angle update happen?

Keep these layers separate. Most of the interesting work belongs in safe, testable code.

Each arrow has to hold up its end of the deal, and the arrows before the maths are the ones that quietly break.

If the snapshot mixed two frames together, flawless trigonometry will aim confidently at where something used to be. If validation mistook a reused entity slot for a live one, the ranking step will happily pick a target that no longer exists. Neither failure looks like a maths error — the numbers are all beautifully computed — which is what makes them hard to find.

Maths cannot repair a bad snapshot. It can only give the wrong answer more decimal places.

Division loses which quadrant the target occupies and behaves badly when the divisor is zero. atan2(y, x) uses both signs and handles axis-aligned directions. hypot measures horizontal distance without choosing one axis. rem_euclid then folds equivalent angles into the range -180 degrees..180 degrees, so crossing the wrap point chooses a two-degree turn instead of a 358-degree turn.

Yaw asks for the direction of the horizontal projection. Pitch asks how far the full vector rises above that projection. Using total 3D distance as the second pitch argument would measure a different angle.

When horizontal equals 0, the target is directly above or below. Pitch remains defined, but yaw is mathematically unconstrained because every horizontal heading points to the same vertical line. Preserve the current yaw in that case rather than letting an arbitrary atan2(0, 0) result cause a snap.

Section: How animation moves a character.

The next section aims at a character’s head, and a head is wherever the animation has put it this frame. An animation describes how some values change over time. The values can be a door’s angle, a button’s colour, the frame of a sprite, or the transforms of a whole skeleton.

Keyframes and interpolation. An animation does not store a value for every frame. It stores values at a few moments, called keyframes, and the engine fills in the rest by interpolating between them (Lesson 4.7). A door keyed at 0 degrees at time 0.0 s and 90 degrees at 1.0 s is at 90 times 0.25 equals 22.5 degrees a quarter of a second in. Add easing and the same moment gives 90 times 0.4375 equals 39.375 degrees with the ease-out curve from Lesson 4.7. Turns are blended as quaternions (Lesson 7.1). A sprite animation is the same idea with whole frames, picked from a sprite sheet by the clock (Lesson 7.2).

Skeletons and skinning. A 3D character is a mesh attached to a skeleton: a hierarchy of bones, each with a transform relative to its parent bone (Lesson 7.1), so turning the upper arm carries the forearm and hand. Every vertex lists up to four bones and a weight for each, and the weights add up to 1. Animation changes the bones’ transforms every frame, and the vertex shader moves each vertex by blending what its bones did. To see the blend, use only movement. A vertex starts at (0, 1, 0) with weight 0.75 on the upper arm and 0.25 on the forearm. In this pose the upper arm moves its points by (2, 0, 0) and the forearm by (6, 0, 0):

The vertex goes mostly where the upper arm goes and is pulled a little toward the forearm, which is why a bent elbow bends smoothly instead of tearing. Real bones use full transforms, not just a move, but the weighted blend is the same. A skeleton of 60 bones, with one 64-byte matrix each (Lesson 7.1), is 60 times 64 equals 3,840 bytes of pose that is rebuilt every frame.

Choosing and blending clips. A character has many clips: idle, walk, run, jump. An animation graph or state machine (Lesson 1.3) decides which clip plays and when to switch. A switch is a cross-fade: for 0.2 s the pose is pose_A times (1 minus w) + pose_B times w, with the weight w rising from 0 to 1, so at 0.1 s it is an even half and half. A mask restricts a clip to some bones, such as a wave that plays on the arm while the legs keep walking. An animation event is a marker at one moment in a clip that runs code, such as the footstep sound 0.3 s into the walk.

Morph targets. A face cannot be posed with bones alone. A morph target, or blend shape, stores the changed positions of vertices for one expression as offsets from the base mesh. A vertex at (0, 0, 0) with a smile offset of (0, 0.2, 0) and a smile weight of 0.5 is drawn at (0, 0.1, 0). Many targets can be mixed at once.

For reading memory, this means a character’s pose is per-frame state, built from a clock value (seconds into the clip) and the bones’ matrices. It is not a fixed fact stored next to the character’s position.

Section: Choose an aim point, not merely an entity origin.

An entity’s position often refers to its feet, collision-cylinder center, or model pivot. A head position may come from a bone transform, tagged attachment point, or fixed offset. Animation changes bone positions each frame.

Code example. The surrounding prose explains this example; the full lesson contains the exact code.

A model is rigged once in a resting posture called the bind pose — arms down, standing straight — and every vertex is authored relative to its bone as that bone sits in that one fixed posture. bone_bind_inverse undoes the bone’s original resting transform, leaving the vertex expressed purely relative to the bone, with the authoring pose divided back out. skeleton_pose then reapplies the bone’s current, animated transform, and entity_world places the whole rig in the level. Skip the bind-inverse step and every point stays glued to the character’s resting pose no matter how the animation actually moves.

The exact matrix order depends on convention. The important distinction is that a bone’s local coordinates are not world coordinates until the hierarchy has been accumulated. A constant z offset may work for one stance and fail while crouching, jumping, or using a different model.
