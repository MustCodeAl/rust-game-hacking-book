Coordinates, Vectors, and Directions

A map tells us which places exist. Coordinates let a tool compare those places: where is the player, how far away is an enemy, and which way does a movement point? Begin with two positions on a grid, then turn their difference into distance and direction. The same operations work in two dimensions or three.

Section: Extend 2D coordinates to three dimensions.

A 2D game often describes a position with x and y.

Image description: A 2D game world

Picture a player at (2, 3) on that map and an enemy at (5, 7). The enemy is 3 units farther along x and 4 along y. Those differences form an arrow from the player to the enemy. Its straight-line length is 5: a right triangle with sides 3 and 4 has a long side of square root of (3 to the power of 2 + 4 to the power of 2) equals square root of 25 equals 5.

A 3D game adds a third axis. If the enemy is also 12 units above the player, the arrow becomes (3, 4, 12) and its length becomes square root of (3 to the power of 2 + 4 to the power of 2 + 12 to the power of 2) equals square root of 169 equals 13. The same subtraction and distance idea grew by one coordinate; no new kind of measurement was required.

That arrow turns positions into useful questions: how far away is a target, which direction should a character move, and is the target inside a guard’s view? We will use the same spatial language for automation and NPC, non player character, decisions. The later camera lessons extend it to projecting the world onto a screen.

Each field is an f32, a 32-bit floating-point number that can hold fractions as well as whole coordinates. #[derive(...)] asks Rust to generate conveniences such as copying and printing this small record; none of those traits changes the geometry. A Vec3 is not special hardware. It is a vector: an ordered group of numbers that we choose to treat as one mathematical object. The order matters because each slot has a role. Swapping x and z changes the meaning even when the three numbers stay the same.

Vectors are useful because one operation can apply to the whole idea. Adding a movement vector to a position moves the position. Subtracting two positions gives the difference between them.

Games disagree about which axis means “up.” Never assume. Move in one direction at a time and observe which field changes.

This is a good example of why a memory layout does not tell the whole story. Three adjacent floats could be a position, velocity, color, scale, or Euler angles. Watch how the game uses them before assigning a name.

Section: Length, normalization, and alignment.

The length of a direction is its distance. Normalizing divides by that length so the result keeps only direction and has length one. Check for a near-zero length first; there is no meaningful direction from a point to itself.

The dot product measures alignment. For two normalized directions it is near 1 when they face the same way, 0 when perpendicular, and -1 when opposite. A field-of-view test can compare the camera’s forward direction with the direction to a target. This avoids turning both vectors into angles merely to ask whether they point roughly the same way.

Section: From geometry to an action.

A position names a place. Subtracting two positions gives a direction with a length; normalizing keeps only the direction. A dot product compares alignment, which lets a decision ask whether a target lies inside a view cone without calculating its angle first. Keep the coordinate meanings and units consistent before using any of these results.
