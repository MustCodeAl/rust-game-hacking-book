♟️ Lesson 4.6Game State, Decisions, and Automation

Coordinates, Vectors, and Directions

Build distance and direction from positions, then use normalization and dot products to express spatial decisions.

About 14 minutes

A map tells us which places exist. Coordinates let a tool compare those places: where is the player, how far away is an enemy, and which way does a movement point? Begin with two positions on a grid, then turn their difference into distance and direction. The same operations work in two dimensions or three.

Extend 2D coordinates to three dimensions

Section titled “Extend 2D coordinates to three dimensions”

A 2D game often describes a position with x and y.

Picture a player at (2, 3) on that map and an enemy at (5, 7). The enemy is 3 units farther along x and 4 along y. Those differences form an arrow from the player to the enemy. Its straight-line length is 5: a right triangle with sides 3 and 4 has a long side of √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5.

A 3D game adds a third axis. If the enemy is also 12 units above the player, the arrow becomes (3, 4, 12) and its length becomes √(3² + 4² + 12²) = √169 = 13. The same subtraction and distance idea grew by one coordinate; no new kind of measurement was required.

That arrow turns positions into useful questions: how far away is a target, which direction should a character move, and is the target inside a guard’s view? We will use the same spatial language for automation and NPC decisions. The later camera lessons extend it to projecting the world onto a screen.

Rust can store the three numbers together:

Each field is an f32, a 32-bit floating-point number that can hold fractions as well as whole coordinates. #[derive(...)] asks Rust to generate conveniences such as copying and printing this small record; none of those traits changes the geometry. A Vec3 is not special hardware. It is a vector: an ordered group of numbers that we choose to treat as one mathematical object. The order matters because each slot has a role. Swapping x and z changes the meaning even when the three numbers stay the same.

Vectors are useful because one operation can apply to the whole idea. Adding a movement vector to a position moves the position. Subtracting two positions gives the difference between them.

Games disagree about which axis means “up.” Never assume. Move in one direction at a time and observe which field changes.

Note

🧭 Coordinate check: change one axis while keeping the others still. The field that follows the movement tells you what that axis means in this game.

Position versus direction

Section titled “Position versus direction”

A position answers “where?” A direction answers “which way?” Both can use three numbers, but they mean different things.

Self inside impl Vec3 means Vec3; self is the vector whose method was called. The subtraction uses enemy minus player, so the result points from the player to the enemy. length() squares each difference, adds the three squares, and takes the square root. These exact coordinates produce the (3, 4, 12) arrow and distance 13 calculated above. Reversing the operands would point the arrow backward while leaving its length unchanged. The :? in println! uses the Debug implementation generated for Vec3, so the program prints all three fields alongside the distance. Put this block after the Vec3 declaration to run the example.

A point and a direction can both be stored as three floats, but the allowed math is different:

Expression Meaning
point − point direction and distance between places
point + direction a new place after moving
direction + direction combined movement
point + point usually has no useful geometric meaning

This is a good example of why a memory layout does not tell the whole story. Three adjacent floats could be a position, velocity, color, scale, or Euler angles. Watch how the game uses them before assigning a name.

Length, normalization, and alignment

Section titled “Length, normalization, and alignment”

The length of a direction is its distance. Normalizing divides by that length so the result keeps only direction and has length one. Check for a near-zero length first; there is no meaningful direction from a point to itself.

For (3, 4, 0), length is 5, so the returned vector is Some((0.6, 0.8, 0)): it points the same way and its length is 1. Option expresses the case where no usable direction exists. When the points coincide, division by zero cannot produce a meaningful aim direction, so the function returns None rather than letting invalid numbers spread into later camera calculations. is_finite also rejects infinity and NaN from a damaged or stale snapshot.

The dot product measures alignment. For two normalized directions it is near 1 when they face the same way, 0 when perpendicular, and -1 when opposite. A field-of-view test can compare the camera’s forward direction with the direction to a target. This avoids turning both vectors into angles merely to ask whether they point roughly the same way.

That second line is why a dot product can become an angle with acos(clamp(dot, -1, 1)). Clamp first because floating-point rounding can produce 1.0000001, which is outside the mathematical input range of acos.

Worked through, a field-of-view test is three multiplications and two additions. Suppose the camera faces straight along positive x, and a target sits ahead and a little to the side:

0.8 is the cosine of roughly 37 degrees, so the target sits about 37 degrees off the centre of view. If the rule is “visible within a 45-degree cone,” you never need acos at all: compare the dot product against cos(45°), which is about 0.707, and anything above that threshold is inside the cone. Comparing cosines is cheaper than converting to angles, and it sidesteps the rounding trap described above.

From geometry to an action

Section titled “From geometry to an action”

A position names a place. Subtracting two positions gives a direction with a length; normalizing keeps only the direction. A dot product compares alignment, which lets a decision ask whether a target lies inside a view cone without calculating its angle first. Keep the coordinate meanings and units consistent before using any of these results.
