Layers, Trust, and Review

Lesson 15.6 — Layers, Trust, and Review: cheatsheet

Another way to think about it

  • Preserve the original event identity across services. Combine its context before deciding how much weight it deserves.

Sections

  1. Know which observation a layer owns
  2. Three reports can describe one event
  3. Matchmaking risk is different from a cheating verdict

Key terms

  • Provenance: Provenance records where information came from and how it was obtained.
  • Correlation: Correlation means observations vary together.
  • trust score: A trust score summarizes risk or expected match quality for a policy.
  • ban wave: A ban wave applies accumulated enforcement decisions in a batch.

Check yourself

A: Two events, after merging the three reports about A

  • Q: Which layer owns accepted inventory and simulation?

A: The authoritative game server

  • Q: What does provenance record?

A: Where information came from and how it was gathered

  • Q: Which details help preserve a forwarded signal's meaning?

A: Originating event and game/detector versions

Made from the lesson text by Game Hacking Academy. Add your own notes below.

A match can have several protection layers. The game validates its rules. A platform provides account and service signals. A league may add its own client requirements and review process. A community server may run plugins with local policies. These layers need not see the same facts or impose the same consequences.

Know which observation a layer owns

Section titled “Know which observation a layer owns”

The game server knows its accepted inventory and simulation. Client protection sees parts of the local software environment. An account service can know historical enforcement records. A replay reviewer sees whatever the recording retained. Each observation has a time, scope, and source.

Provenance records where information came from and how it was obtained. Keep the originating event identifier, game version, server state, and detector version when forwarding a signal. Otherwise, a later service may misinterpret a copied report as a new measurement.

Three reports can describe one event

Section titled “Three reports can describe one event”

Label a toy event A. The game logs it, a plugin forwards it, and a player report references it. That creates three notifications. Their common origin means they still describe one event.

A later event B adds another observation. Now there are two distinct events. Distinct does not automatically mean statistically independent: both might have been affected by the same game bug or network condition.

Correlation means observations vary together. When several detectors share inputs or assumptions, their mistakes can occur together too. Count unique events and evaluate the combined policy on legitimate cases. Do not turn a copied signal into extra confidence merely by routing it through additional services.

Alternative: Preserve the original event identity across services. Combine its context before deciding how much weight it deserves.

Matchmaking risk is different from a cheating verdict

Section titled “Matchmaking risk is different from a cheating verdict”

A trust score summarizes risk or expected match quality for a policy. It may guide matchmaking or prioritize review. It is not necessarily an assertion that the account cheated in a particular match.

Valve’s published CS:GO Trust Factor explanation illustrates that distinction: it describes matchmaking using account and play observations, while leaving changing details undisclosed. An account score does not replace a rule-specific explanation for an enforcement decision.

New accounts, shared hardware, changing devices, and unusual but permitted play can complicate such signals. Keep the scope of a consequence tied to the evidence and documented rules. A community server’s local removal is different from a platform-wide sanction.

Delayed enforcement has tradeoffs

Section titled “Delayed enforcement has tradeoffs”

A ban wave applies accumulated enforcement decisions in a batch. Grouping cases can give investigators time to validate them and make immediate detector feedback less revealing. The delay also allows further harm unless other controls prevent or mitigate it.

Use durable case records and preserve the rule version that made each decision. If an update causes a false-positive cluster, pause the affected enforcement rule, investigate its shared cause, and correct affected cases. A general recovery process should exist before a large batch is applied.

Review queues need clear prioritization and limited data access. A report can be valuable without being sufficient evidence for a ban. Repeated reports from the same source or the same incident should not automatically increase the penalty. The purpose of layering is to add useful coverage and context, not to hide responsibility behind a majority of flags.
